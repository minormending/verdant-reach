# Premise

## The Quickening

*Quick* is the old English word for *alive* ("the quick and the dead"). Plants
that woke up are **Quickened**. The night it happened is **the Quickening**.

### What people think happened (Act 1)

The **Centuryheart** is a giant plant in the high valley. It blooms once
roughly every hundred years and then dies (it's modelled on *Puya raimondii*,
the Queen of the Andes). It finally flowered. That night its pollen drifted
down the valley, and by morning certain plants were moving.

Everyone assumes the bloom caused the Quickening. Folklore agrees: the old
songs say "the heart blooms and the woods listen."

### What actually happened (revealed in Act 2)

The region sits on top of **the Elder Grove**, a single clonal organism. It's
thousands of trunks sharing one root system, like the real aspen colony Pando,
and it's tied into every other root in the valley by fungal threads. Plants
really do warn each other chemically when they're attacked, and they may pass
signals through those fungal networks. The game turns that idea up to eleven.

Months before the bloom, **Rootstock** began grafting into the Elder's roots,
trying to tap the network. The Elder felt it as an attack and **raised an
alarm**. The Centuryheart's bloom carried that alarm on the air. Any plant
close enough to the network's strongest nodes, and receptive enough, woke up
to defend itself.

**So the Quickening is an immune response, and the villains caused it.** That
explains several things:

- **Why only specific plants woke:** proximity to network hubs, and species
  that already "listen" well (the ones with deep fungal partnerships).
- **Why the Quickened are territorial and quick to fight:** they woke up
  *defending*.
- **Why Rootstock is so interested:** they caused it, and they want to finish
  what they started.

## Rules of the world

1. **Only some plants are Quickened.** Most of the world is ordinary
   vegetation. A Quickened plant is noticeable: it turns to face you, its
   leaves move against the wind, it relocates overnight.
2. **They're plants first.** They need light, water and soil. They're slow
   in cold, strong in sun (or in moonlight, for night bloomers), and they
   grow through their real life stages.
3. **They can move, slowly.** Most shuffle on their roots or drag themselves
   along with tendrils. That's why they live in tall grass, hedges, marshes
   and canopies rather than open road.
4. **No faces.** Sentience shows through movement: turning, curling,
   snapping, unfurling, drooping. (This is also an art-pipeline constraint;
   see decision Q3 in 07.)
5. **They choose.** A Quickened plant can't be forced into a pod. It accepts
   one when it's weakened or curious, or when it's grown from seed by you.
6. **Battles are contests, not violence.** Plants have always competed for
   light, water and space. Quickened plants do it face to face. A beaten
   plant **wilts** and recovers with water and light. Nobody dies.
7. **Some things aren't plants.** Mushrooms, kelp and lichen aren't Quickened.
   They're the network, the sea and the in-between (decision Q7: they're scenery, never creatures).

## Themes

| Theme | How it shows up |
|---|---|
| **Diversity vs. control** | Rootstock wants one rootstock under everything: a monoculture that obeys. The real-world echo is the Gros Michel banana, wiped out by a single disease because every plant was a clone. |
| **Patience and growth** | Nothing in the game is instant. Evolution follows real growth. The legendary bloomed once in a century. |
| **Stewardship, not ownership** | You *tend* Quickened plants; they choose to come with you. The rival starts out thinking of them as tools. |
| **Curiosity as a virtue** | You're a scientist. Completing the Field Herbarium *is* the job, and every entry teaches a true fact. |

## Tone

Cozy wonder with a spine. Think Crystal crossed with a nature documentary.
Most of the game is warm and curious, and the threat is real but never grim.
Every Quickened plant's herbarium entry should hold one true, surprising fact
about the real plant. That's the hook that makes "real plants" pay off.
